Domain Analysis¶

The business domain problem centers on effectively analyzing and managing National Health Service (NHS) healthcare operations data. These operations and associated data include:

  • Appointment Records
  • Billing Records
  • Insurance Records
  • Prescription Records
  • Surgery Records
  • Test Records

Given the scale of the NHS and the complex, critical nature of the UK healthcare system — combined with poor data integration — there are significant inefficiencies, miscommunications, and resource misallocations. These issues negatively affect both the cost of healthcare and overall patient satisfaction.

In this context, the application of data science is particularly compelling. Data science allows unnormalized and fragmented data to be transformed into structured, analyzable information. By applying techniques such as data normalization, pattern recognition, and statistical analysis, healthcare providers can extract essential insights to streamline workflows and improve service delivery nationwide.

Main Areas of Interest¶

The key areas identified for investigation include:

  • Understanding relationships between hospitals, services, medical professionals, and patients
  • Identifying patterns in appointments, service usage, and patient preferences
  • Improving the planning and scheduling of healthcare services

Potential Solutions¶

A potential solution to this domain problem involves developing a normalized database of NHS operational data. This would:

  • Promote data integrity and consistency
  • Enable advanced analysis such as predictive modeling
  • Support the creation of dashboards for administrative use
  • Optimize scheduling of health services
  • Automate reporting
  • Facilitate real-time querying for evidence-based decision-making across the organization

Inferential Statistics¶

Problem:¶

Identify factors that influence whether a patient misses (cancels) an appointment. The aim is to improve scheduling and reduce wasted clinical resources.

Note on the outcome: the database records three statuses: Completed (450,595), Cancelled (50,235) and Scheduled (55,862). There is no No-Show status, so a cancelled appointment is used as the missed-appointment outcome. Scheduled appointments have not happened yet and are excluded.

Solution¶

Chi-square tests of independence between appointment status (Cancelled vs Completed) and each categorical factor: age group (age at the time of the appointment), gender, weekday, appointment hour, department and hospital. SQL extracts the data and pandas/scipy run the tests. Cramér's V measures effect size, because with ~500k rows even tiny differences can look significant.

Justification¶

The chi-square test is the standard test of association between two categorical variables, and Cramér's V shows whether an association is large enough to matter in practice.

Implementation¶

import sqlite3
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

conn = sqlite3.connect('NHSDBB.DB')

query = """
SELECT
    a.Appointment_Status,
    p.Patient_Gender,
    CASE
        WHEN CAST(strftime('%Y', a.Appointment_Date) AS INT) - CAST(strftime('%Y', p.Patient_Date_Of_Birth) AS INT) < 18 THEN 'Child'
        WHEN CAST(strftime('%Y', a.Appointment_Date) AS INT) - CAST(strftime('%Y', p.Patient_Date_Of_Birth) AS INT) <= 65 THEN 'Adult'
        ELSE 'Senior'
    END AS Age_Group,
    h.Hospital_Name,
    d.Department_Name,
    CAST(strftime('%w', a.Appointment_Date) AS INT) AS Weekday,
    CAST(substr(a.Appointment_Time, 1, 2) AS INT) AS Hour
FROM Appointment a
JOIN Patient p ON a.Patient_ID = p.Patient_ID
JOIN Hospital h ON a.Hospital_ID = h.Hospital_ID
JOIN Department d ON a.Department_ID = d.Department_ID
WHERE a.Appointment_Status IN ('Cancelled', 'Completed');
"""

conn.close()

print(f"Appointments analysed: {len(data):,}")
print(f"Overall cancellation rate: {(data['Appointment_Status'] == 'Cancelled').mean():.2%}\n")

# Chi-square test + Cramér's V for each factor
results = []
for factor in ['Age_Group', 'Patient_Gender', 'Weekday', 'Hour', 'Department_Name', 'Hospital_Name']:
    table = pd.crosstab(data[factor], data['Appointment_Status'])
    chi2, p, dof, _ = chi2_contingency(table)
    v = np.sqrt(chi2 / (table.values.sum() * (min(table.shape) - 1)))
    results.append({'Factor': factor, 'Chi2': round(chi2, 2), 'dof': dof, 'p_value': round(p, 4), 'Cramers_V': round(v, 4)})
print(pd.DataFrame(results).to_string(index=False))

# Cancellation rate by age group and gender
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4.5))
for ax, factor in zip(axes, ['Age_Group', 'Patient_Gender']):

    rate.plot(kind='bar', ax=ax, color='#4C72B0')
    ax.set_title(f'Cancellation rate by {factor}')
    ax.set_ylabel('% of appointments cancelled')
    ax.set_xlabel('')
    ax.tick_params(axis='x', rotation=0)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Of 500,830 completed or cancelled appointments, 10.0% were cancelled. None of the factors tested is significantly associated with cancellation (all p > 0.2), and every Cramér's V is below 0.03, so the effect sizes are negligible. Limitations¶

The data has no true No-Show status, so cancellations are used as a proxy. The test only detects pairwise associations and does not control for confounding factors. The data appears to be synthetic, which would explain why status is independent of every attribute.

Supervised Learning¶

Problem¶

Predict whether an appointment will be cancelled before it happens, so that high-risk slots could be overbooked or the patient sent a reminder.

Solution¶

A logistic regression classifier trained on gender, age group, hospital, department, weekday and appointment hour. The categorical features are one-hot encoded. Because only 10% of appointments are cancelled, class_weight='balanced' is used, and the model is evaluated with ROC-AUC and per-class recall rather than accuracy.

Justification¶

Logistic regression is an interpretable baseline for binary classification. ROC-AUC shows whether the model ranks risk better than chance (0.5), which accuracy cannot show on imbalanced data.

Unsupervised Learning¶

Problem¶

Segment patients into groups based on their healthcare usage patterns, so that services and outreach can be personalised.

Solution:¶

K-means clustering on three standardised patient-level features: appointment frequency, age, and personal cancellation rate. The number of clusters k is chosen with the silhouette score instead of being fixed in advance.

Justification:¶

K-means is efficient on 400,000 patients and suits finding patterns in unlabelled data. Standardising the features stops any one of them from dominating the distance calculation, and the silhouette score gives an objective way to choose k.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

conn = sqlite3.connect('NHSDBB.DB')

query = """
SELECT
    p.Patient_ID,
    COUNT(a.Appointment_ID) AS Appointment_Frequency,
    2025 - CAST(strftime('%Y', p.Patient_Date_Of_Birth) AS INT) AS Age,
    AVG(a.Appointment_Status = 'Cancelled') AS Cancel_Rate
FROM Appointment a
JOIN Patient p ON a.Patient_ID = p.Patient_ID
GROUP BY p.Patient_ID;
"""
data = pd.read_sql_query(query, conn)
conn.close()

features = ['Appointment_Frequency', 'Age', 'Cancel_Rate']
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(data[features])

# Choose k with the silhouette score (computed on a 20k sample for speed)
sample = np.random.default_rng(42).choice(len(X), 20000, replace=False)
scores = {}
for k in range(2, 7):
    labels = KMeans(n_clusters=k, random_state=42, n_init=10).fit_predict(X)
    scores[k] = silhouette_score(X[sample], labels[sample])
print('Silhouette scores:', {k: round(s, 3) for k, s in scores.items()})
best_k = max(scores, key=scores.get)
print('Chosen k =', best_k)

data['Cluster'] = KMeans(n_clusters=best_k, random_state=42, n_init=10).fit_predict(X)
profile = data.groupby('Cluster')[features].mean().round(2)
profile['Patients'] = data['Cluster'].value_counts().sort_index()
